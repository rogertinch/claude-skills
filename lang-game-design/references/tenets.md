# Eric M. Lang's Tenets of Good TCG / Card Game Design

> Compiled from interviews, essays, podcast appearances, and his 2015 "TCG Design 101" Twitter thread.
> Eric M. Lang is the designer of A Game of Thrones CCG, Call of Cthulhu CCG, Star Wars: The Card Game LCG, Warhammer: Invasion LCG, Warhammer 40K: Conquest LCG, Blood Rage, Rising Sun, Ankh, Chaos in the Old World, and 100+ other titles. Recipient of the 2016 Diana Jones Award.

---

## The TCG Design 101 Rules

From a 2015 Twitter thread, later compiled on BoardGameGeek's "Designing Cardboard" blog:

### 1. Cards Are the Game, Not the Rules
> "Always be willing to sacrifice tactical depth in the core rules to make room for increased depth of card interaction."

The base rules of a TCG should be a simple chassis. Complexity and strategic depth should emerge from how cards interact with each other, not from the rulebook itself. If you're adding depth to the core rules, you're stealing space from your cards.

### 2. Victory Conditions and Resource Engines Must Be Decoupled
> "Your game's victory condition should not be tied to your core resource engine."

The thing you spend to play cards and the thing that determines who wins should not be the same system. If they are, the game collapses into a single optimization axis — whoever manages that one resource best wins. Separate systems create tension: do I invest in my engine or push toward victory?

### 3. Elegant Power Differentiation
> "Look for the most elegant mechanic to differentiate power level between cards. Cost to deploy is one of the most tried and true."

Don't invent elaborate subsystems to make some cards stronger than others. A simple mana/cost curve is proven. The elegance is the point — players need to instantly read relative power from the card's face.

### 4. Simple Victory Conditions
> "More than any other type of game, keep your victory conditions simple."

In TCGs, the cards themselves generate enough complexity. If your win condition is complicated, players can't evaluate whether they're ahead or behind, and every card interaction becomes harder to assess. Simple win conditions let card effects feel meaningful.

### 5. Use Factions to Constrain Deckbuilding
> "Split your card pool into factions or something equally elegant to meaningfully restrict players' deck-building choices."

Unrestricted card pools make deckbuilding overwhelming and collapse toward solved "best decks." Faction restrictions create meaningful constraints that generate strategic depth — the limitation itself is the design tool. Players must make tradeoffs, and those tradeoffs create identity.

### 6. Essential TCG Vocabulary
Every TCG designer must understand: **Curve, Aggro, Midrange, Combo, Control, Ramp, Tempo, RNG, Value, Card Advantage.** These are the vocabulary of the medium. You can subvert them, but you need to understand them first.

Lang also pointed designers to Mark Rosewater's design theory blog and the Hearthstone GDC talk as supplementary reading.

## The Foundational Philosophy

From interviews, essays, and podcast appearances:

### 7. Complex Interactions from Simple Pieces
> "My strength lies in complex interactions from simple pieces, and a design sculpted to feel scary and open-ended, even within a tight rules set."

> "Most of the complexity from these pieces is emergent rather than spelled out."

This is Lang's self-described signature. He designs "exceptions-based games where the rules are essentially a toolkit to create exciting interactions between components with abilities." In customizable card games specifically: "Most of the fun is finding combos and synergy between cards. I do my best to design card abilities with a firm logical framework, but open-ended enough where I as designer cannot possibly see all ends. **If I can be surprised by player discoveries in the game, then I believe I have succeeded.**"

*Source: The Opinionated Gamers interview (2012)*

### 8. Player Empowerment Over Designer Cleverness
> "I generally prefer more open-ended games, where I as the player feel enabled by the game to architect my own success, and feel empowered by my own cleverness rather than celebrate the designer's."

The player should feel like *they* won through their own ingenuity — not that they found the solution the designer hid. Chaos in the Old World exemplifies this: it "uses a fairly rudimentary rules base to encourage players to step outside the 'script' and find their own creative ways to win."

*Source: The Opinionated Gamers interview (2012)*

### 9. Design for Payoff, Not Input
> "Games are designed for payoff, not for input."

Lang pushes back on the idea that "agonizing decisions" are inherently good. Players value the emotional payoff of a mechanic more than the mechanic itself. The most enduring games minimize friction while maximizing emotional impact. Reduce effort to reach the magic moment — don't worship complexity for its own sake.

*Source: Think Like a Game Designer, Episode 100 (2026)*

### 10. Psychology Is the Designer's Core Discipline
> "From [Sid Sackson's] games and writing I decided that psychology is the most important field a designer can master."

> "The best designs don't dictate interaction, they inspire it."

> "It's essential to have a rock solid grasp of mechanics, game theory and behavioral psychology — they are the language of game design; they connect your ideas and the player experience."

A TCG isn't a math puzzle — it's a psychological contest. The rules are a medium for human interaction. Understanding why players make choices matters more than understanding the choices themselves.

For a game design curriculum, Lang would focus on: "Game history, Psychology, Anthropology, Sociology, Contemporary World History, Applied Mathematics (focused on statistics, probability, and algebra), Architecture, Linguistics, and Marketing."

*Source: The Opinionated Gamers interview (2012)*

### 11. Design the Emotional Experience
> "Everything about Blood Rage is manipulation. I'm designing an experience, where I want players to act a certain way."

> "I have the game designer's weakness. In any social situation, I'm deeply uncomfortable when people aren't having fun."

Lang understands "how the dopamine cycle works" and every part of the game — fun or not — is intentional. He designed the Game of Thrones engine in one day, iterating with sugar packets "until he could get an emotional reaction out of himself." The core principle: game design is about crafting an emotional experience. If a mechanic doesn't generate a feeling, it doesn't belong.

*Source: Dice Tower Dish interview series (2022); Think Like a Game Designer #17*

### 12. Player Psychology Matters More Than Mechanics
> "Player psychology matters more than mechanics alone."

Competitive and emotionally resonant are not opposites. The best card games create tension through uncertainty, social reading, and stakes — not through mechanical complexity. If your game is strategically sound but emotionally flat, it will fail.

*Source: Think Like a Game Designer, Episodes 17 and 100*

### 13. Hand Management Over Board Management
> The gameplay should be "more hand management than straightforward resource management... You are more focused on when and how to use your cards, than how to squeeze maximum value out of your board position."

For card games specifically: the interesting decisions should live in your hand — *when* to play, not just *what* to play. Timing, sequencing, and reading the opponent matter more than optimal board-state calculation. He wanted to achieve this "in a simple, intuitive way that did not bog the players down in rules exceptions, but rather let the game itself stand aside and allow card interactions to take center stage."

*Source: Theology of Games interview on Star Wars: The Card Game (2012)*

### 14. Variance Is a Feature, Not a Bug
> "The same two decks will play differently from game to game, and all mechanics were designed to offer this experience."

> "The element of luck in the game forces variance and adaptability without (usually) simply smashing anyone out of contention."

A card game with no variance is a solved game. Randomness forces adaptation, rewards flexibility, and ensures that the experience stays fresh. The key is to make variance interact with skill — not replace it.

*Source: Theology of Games interview (2012); Opinionated Gamers on Chaos in the Old World*

### 15. The "Complexity Budget"
> "Removing all of our presupposed game design knowledge, making sure that we were always aware of where the complexity was being added."

Lang uses a literal "complexity budget" — every mechanic costs against a finite allowance. Unnecessary complexity is "homework — the type of set-up, administrative and puzzly complexity that some hardcore gamers love but most gamers do not." A game can have the variety and replayability of top hobby games while spending its complexity budget wisely.

*Source: Mojo Nation — Life in Reterra Interview; GamesRadar+ (2024)*

### 16. Game Design as Philosophy
> "Game design is neither art nor science; it is rather a philosophy, a way of looking at the world through the lens of structured interaction."

> "Game design is a filter through which you view the world. How do we codify interaction between people in a multitude of settings? How do we align motivation, competition, cooperation and perception into play patterns?"

Designers must be multi-disciplinarians — "adept at psychology, economics, communication, math, marketing, sociology… arguably even history" — and must be "at once blindly creative and shrewdly analytical."

*Source: The Opinionated Gamers interview (2012); attributed influence from Richard Garfield*

## On Licensed / Thematic Design

### 17. Respect the Medium
> "Respect the medium."

When adapting a story into a card game, you are not simulating the story — you are making a card game that evokes the story's *feeling*. Collectible card games are "at their root combinatorial exercises; players fall in love with possibilities as much as strategies, and no other type of game offers more options."

Don't try to make a card game do what a board game or RPG does better. Lean into what cards are uniquely good at: combination, surprise, and possibility space.

*Source: "Design Decisions and Concepts in Licensed Collectible Card Games," Electronic Book Review (2008)*

### 18. Use "Loaded Terms" for Thematic Resonance
> The term "kneel" to indicate a card has been temporarily used — a mechanic normally called "tap" — "strongly evoked a medieval air and brought to mind the many occasions in the series where characters 'bend the knee' to signal obedience."

Naming matters enormously. Turn phases named Plot, Draw, Marshalling, Challenges, Dominance, and Stand "strongly implied a narrative flow, so that a new player would subconsciously approach the game expecting medieval conflict and intrigue." The language of your game teaches players how to *feel* about it before they understand the rules.

*Source: Electronic Book Review essay (2008)*

### 19. Embrace Impossible Combinations
> "As long as players could conceive of the possibility that an event might take place in the world of Westeros, he had to make sure it could be done in the game — and in many cases, things that could never happen in the books could also be done."

A card game should let players explore *what if* scenarios that the source material never allows. This is the unique fantasy of the medium. Don't constrain your game to canon — let the combinatorial space tell new stories.

*Source: Electronic Book Review essay (2008)*

## On Asymmetric Faction Design

### 20. Factions Must Feel Fundamentally Different to Play
> Each god's mechanics in Chaos in the Old World are "profound, and fundamentally change the way you'll find yourself playing each faction."

A faction isn't a reskin with different numbers. Each faction should demand a different strategic mindset. Players should feel like they're playing a different game depending on their faction — not the same game with different paint.

Chaos in the Old World exemplifies this: Nurgle spreads corruption, Tzeentch manipulates magic cards, Slaanesh adapts, Khorne kills. Each god wants different things from the same board state, creating natural conflict without needing direct combat incentives.

### 21. Balance Asymmetry Through Experience, Not Math
> Asymmetric games "require delicate balance so that either side has a fair chance to win."

> "Until you have a grasp of all of the factions, how can you hope to compete?"

True balance in an asymmetric game can only be evaluated by players who understand all sides. The first several games are learning games by design — and that's fine. Asymmetric balance is validated through mastery, not first impressions.

### 22. Static Asymmetry vs. Dynamic Asymmetry
**Static asymmetry** (Chaos in the Old World): each faction has a fixed identity from the start. Deep, replayable, but can create player-count balance issues.

**Dynamic asymmetry** (Blood Rage): factions start symmetric but diverge through player choice (card drafting). More flexible, scales better across player counts, but requires the draft system to offer meaningful differentiation.

> "Blood Rage creates asymmetry through card choice... each clan will quickly take on a personality that is distinct from the others in every game."

Lang moved from static to dynamic asymmetry across his career — not because one is better, but because each fits different design needs.

## On the Creative Process

### 23. Theme First, Mechanics Serve
> "A game begins with a vision, inspiration or an intended audience. That foundation creates a specific set of needs. The design process is crafting the game to meet those needs."

> "I tend to be inspired by metaphors... I rarely design a game simply because of a mechanic."

Start with the feeling you want to create, then find mechanics that deliver that feeling. Not the reverse.

### 24. Prototype Ugly, Prototype Fast
> "Splenda sugar packets are my CCG game design tool of choice."

When designing the Game of Thrones CCG engine, Lang spent a full day iterating with nothing but sugar packets — playing his prototype over and over "until he could get an emotional reaction out of himself." If you can't feel the game with sugar packets, adding art won't save it.

### 25. Iterate and Cut Ruthlessly
> "Great games come from iteration and cutting what doesn't serve the design."

> "All games suck until they don't."

Every mechanic must earn its place. If a system doesn't directly serve the game's emotional or strategic core, remove it. Don't add — subtract until only the essential remains.

### 26. Simplicity Is Not Dumbing Down
> "The idea of mass market 'being simple' is bullshit."

Simple rules and deep play are not opposites. The best games have rules you can teach in minutes and strategy that takes years to master. Complexity should come from emergence, not from the rulebook.

*Source: GamesRadar interview, UK Games Expo (2024)*

## On Ritual Play and Longevity

### 27. The Danger of Total Control
> "The principal mistake was that the designer gave me 100% control."

With full creative control, "knowing when you're making mistakes is hard." Lang emphasizes the importance of collaborators who can tell you when and why you're wrong. Everyone on the team should get a voice; the lead designer makes final decisions, but the key is "having the honesty to admit when a team member's idea works better than your own."

*Source: Think Like a Game Designer, Episode 17 (2019)*

### 28. Not Every Game Needs a Mastery Curve
> "I want to play this, but I don't want to get better at it. This feels like something I just want to do as a group meditation."

Some games are valuable precisely because they resist improvement. They become shared habits — social anchors people return to without pressure. Consider whether your game is meant to be mastered or meant to be *returned to*.

*Source: Think Like a Game Designer, Episode 100 (2026)*

## Summary: Lang's Design Tenets Applied to TMDBattle

| Lang Tenet | TMDBattle Application |
|------------|----------------------|
| Cards are the game, not the rules | Keep the action matrix simple (attack/defend/special). Let card abilities, chemistry, and crew passives create depth. |
| Decouple victory from resources | Don't let the same stat that determines damage also determine who wins the match. HP (survival) and Impact (damage) are already separate — preserve this. |
| Elegant power differentiation | Rarity tier + simple stat scaling is the right approach. Don't add elaborate subsystems to make legendaries feel special — the numbers should speak. |
| Simple victory conditions | "Last team standing" is correct. Don't complicate it with point systems or alternative win conditions. |
| Complex interactions from simple pieces | Cast charisma + crew oeuvre + chemistry + type matchups should create emergent combos the designer didn't predict. If players can surprise us, we've succeeded. |
| Player empowerment over designer cleverness | Don't script outcomes. Let players discover that a low-rarity cast card with perfect chemistry outperforms a legendary with none. |
| Design for payoff, not input | Cast/crew mechanics should create *moments* — a clutch ability trigger, a chemistry bonus turning a losing fight. Not bookkeeping. |
| Psychology over mechanics | The action-commit system (simultaneous reveal) is inherently psychological. Lean into the read/bluff dynamic rather than adding more mechanical options. |
| Hand management > board management | If cast "cameo" interrupts are added, they should feel like hand management — *when* to spend your cast card, not whether. |
| Variance is a feature | Chemistry and type matchups already create variance between identical decks. Crew cooldowns or cast triggers would add more session-to-session variety. |
| Respect the medium | TMDBattle is a card game about movies, not a movie simulator. Lean into combinatorial fantasy — what if Kubrick directed a Marvel actor? |
| Loaded terms for thematic resonance | Name abilities and mechanics after film industry language: "Direct," "Score," "Cut," "Greenlight," "Recast." |
| Embrace impossible combinations | Let players pair any actor with any movie. The "what if" fantasy of impossible casts is the unique joy of the medium. |
| Factions feel fundamentally different | If crew roles become archetypes, each should create a genuinely different playstyle — not just +5% to different stats. |
| Dynamic > static asymmetry for TMDBattle | Deck composition (crew choice + movie types) should create asymmetry through player choice, not fixed faction assignment. |
| Use factions to constrain deckbuilding | Crew role as faction identity naturally restricts viable deck compositions. Type-matching bonuses further constrain without explicit rules. |
| The complexity budget | Every new cast/crew mechanic costs complexity. Budget wisely — a charisma stat modifier costs almost nothing; a cast interrupt system costs a lot. |
| Design the emotional experience | The clutch moment when a chemistry-matched cast card triggers Star Power at 30% HP — that's the payoff. Design backward from that feeling. |
| Iterate and cut ruthlessly | Start with the simplest cast/crew changes (charisma stat modifier + oeuvre scaling). Only add new actions/abilities if the simple version isn't enough. |
