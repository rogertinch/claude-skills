# Laws of UX — Quick Reference

Source: [lawsofux.com](https://lawsofux.com)

---

## Aesthetic-Usability Effect
**Tagline**: Users often perceive aesthetically pleasing design as design that's more usable.
- Aesthetically pleasing design creates positive response and leads users to believe it works better.
- People are more tolerant of minor usability issues in visually pleasing designs.
- Visual polish can mask usability problems and hide issues during testing.

## Choice Overload
**Tagline**: The tendency for people to get overwhelmed when presented with a large number of options.
- Too many options hurts decision-making and degrades the overall experience.
- Enable side-by-side comparison when comparison is necessary.
- Prioritize content shown at any moment; use search/filtering to narrow options up front.

## Chunking
**Tagline**: Individual pieces of information are broken down and grouped together in a meaningful whole.
- Chunking lets users scan content and identify relevant information quickly.
- Structure content into visually distinct groups with clear hierarchy.
- Chunking reveals relationships by grouping content into modules with clear separation rules.

## Cognitive Bias
**Tagline**: A systematic error of thinking that influences perception and decision-making.
- People use mental shortcuts (heuristics) based on past experiences to make quick decisions.
- These shortcuts can introduce errors without the person's awareness.
- Awareness of biases (e.g. confirmation bias) helps guard against them in design decisions.

## Cognitive Load
**Tagline**: The amount of mental resources needed to understand and interact with an interface.
- When incoming information exceeds capacity, tasks become harder and details are missed.
- Intrinsic load: effort to hold goal-relevant information in mind.
- Extraneous load: unnecessary mental processing caused by distracting/irrelevant design elements.

## Doherty Threshold
**Tagline**: Productivity soars when a computer and its users interact at a pace (<400ms) that ensures neither has to wait.
- Provide system feedback within 400ms to keep users' attention.
- Use perceived performance techniques (skeleton screens, optimistic UI) to reduce wait perception.
- Animations, progress bars, and loading states help make wait times tolerable.

## Fitts's Law
**Tagline**: The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to and size of the target.
- Touch targets should be large enough for accurate selection.
- Targets need ample spacing between them to avoid mis-taps.
- Place targets in areas that are easy to reach (bottom of screen for thumbs, near cursor paths).

## Flow
**Tagline**: A mental state of energized focus and full involvement in an activity.
- Flow requires a balance between task difficulty and user skill level.
- Tasks too hard cause frustration; tasks too easy cause boredom.
- Design for flow: clear feedback, efficiency, minimal friction, discoverable content.

## Goal-Gradient Effect
**Tagline**: The tendency to approach a goal increases with proximity to the goal.
- Users work faster the closer they are to completion.
- Artificial progress (e.g. pre-filled progress bars, head-start rewards) increases motivation.
- Always show clear progress indicators to keep users motivated.

## Hick's Law
**Tagline**: The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices.
- Minimize choices when response time is critical.
- Break complex tasks into smaller steps to reduce cognitive load.
- Highlight recommended options to guide decisions. Use progressive onboarding.
- Simplify, but don't abstract to the point of removing necessary options.

## Jakob's Law
**Tagline**: Users prefer your site to work the same way as all the other sites they already know.
- Users transfer expectations from familiar products to new similar ones.
- Leverage existing mental models so users focus on tasks, not on learning new patterns.
- When breaking conventions, let users opt in to familiar patterns during transitions.

## Law of Common Region
**Tagline**: Elements tend to be perceived as groups if they share an area with a clearly defined boundary.
- Common region creates structure and helps users understand relationships between elements.
- Borders and backgrounds are easy ways to establish common region.

## Law of Prägnanz
**Tagline**: People interpret ambiguous or complex images as the simplest form possible.
- The eye seeks simplicity and order to avoid cognitive overload.
- Simple figures are better processed and remembered than complex ones.
- Complex shapes are mentally simplified into unified forms.

## Law of Proximity
**Tagline**: Objects that are near each other tend to be grouped together.
- Proximity establishes relationships between nearby elements.
- Close elements are perceived to share similar functionality or traits.
- Proper spacing helps users organize and understand information efficiently.

## Law of Similarity
**Tagline**: The human eye perceives similar elements as a complete picture or group, even when separated.
- Visually similar elements are perceived as related.
- Color, shape, size, orientation, and movement signal group membership.
- Links and navigation must be visually differentiated from plain text.

## Law of Uniform Connectedness
**Tagline**: Visually connected elements are perceived as more related than elements with no connection.
- Group related functions using visual connections: colors, lines, frames, or shapes.
- Tangible connectors (arrows, lines) can also create visual relationships.
- Use connectedness to show context and emphasize relationships.

## Mental Model
**Tagline**: A compressed model based on what we think we know about a system and how it works.
- Users apply working models from familiar products to new similar ones.
- Match designs to users' mental models to improve experience and reduce learning.
- Shrinking the gap between your mental model and users' is a core UX challenge.

## Miller's Law
**Tagline**: The average person can only keep 7 (plus or minus 2) items in their working memory.
- Don't use "7 items" to justify unnecessary design limits.
- Chunk content into smaller groups to aid processing and memory.
- Short-term memory capacity varies by individual, prior knowledge, and context.

## Occam's Razor
**Tagline**: Among equally predictive hypotheses, the one with the fewest assumptions should be selected.
- The best way to reduce complexity is to avoid creating it in the first place.
- Analyze and remove every element possible without compromising function.
- A design is complete when nothing else can be removed.

## Paradox of the Active User
**Tagline**: Users never read manuals but start using software immediately.
- Users prioritize completing tasks immediately over learning the system first.
- This is irrational long-term but rational short-term; designers must accommodate it.
- Make guidance contextual, discoverable in-flow (tooltips, empty states, progressive disclosure).

## Pareto Principle
**Tagline**: Roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes.
- Inputs and outputs are rarely evenly distributed.
- Focus effort on the areas that deliver the greatest benefit to the most users.
- Design for the most common use cases first.

## Parkinson's Law
**Tagline**: Any task will inflate until all the available time is spent.
- Limit task completion time to match user expectations.
- Reducing perceived and actual task duration improves experience.
- Use autofill, smart defaults, and pre-population to prevent task inflation.

## Peak-End Rule
**Tagline**: People judge an experience based on how they felt at its peak and at its end.
- Pay closest attention to the most intense moments and the final moments of user journeys.
- Design peak moments to delight; design endings to satisfy.
- Negative experiences are recalled more vividly than positive ones.

## Postel's Law
**Tagline**: Be liberal in what you accept, and conservative in what you send.
- Be empathetic and flexible about user input, access, and behavior.
- Anticipate and handle diverse input gracefully; provide clear feedback.
- Resilient design accepts variable input and translates it to meet system requirements.

## Selective Attention
**Tagline**: The process of focusing attention on a subset of stimuli, usually those related to our goals.
- Users filter out irrelevant information; designers must guide attention to what matters.
- Banner blindness: users ignore ad-like content. Avoid styling content to look like ads.
- Change blindness: significant UI changes go unnoticed without strong cues. Signal important changes clearly.

## Serial Position Effect
**Tagline**: Users best remember the first and last items in a series.
- Place least important items in the middle of lists.
- Position key actions at the far left and far right of navigation.

## Tesler's Law
**Tagline**: Every system has a certain amount of complexity that cannot be reduced — it must be borne by either the system or the user.
- All processes have irreducible complexity; design must absorb it before the user does.
- Lift as much burden as possible from users through design and development.
- Don't design for an idealized rational user; account for real, irrational behavior.

## Von Restorff Effect
**Tagline**: When multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs is most likely to be remembered.
- Make important information and key actions visually distinctive.
- Use emphasis sparingly to avoid visual competition.
- Don't rely solely on color for contrast — consider color vision deficiency and low vision users.

## Working Memory
**Tagline**: A cognitive system that temporarily holds and manipulates information needed to complete tasks.
- Working memory holds 4–7 chunks; each fades after 20–30 seconds.
- Support recognition over recall: show what's been seen (visited links, breadcrumbs).
- Carry critical information across screens so users don't need to memorize it.

## Zeigarnik Effect
**Tagline**: People remember uncompleted or interrupted tasks better than completed tasks.
- Invite content discovery with clear signifiers of additional content.
- Artificial progress toward a goal motivates users to complete it.
- Provide clear progress indicators throughout multi-step tasks.
